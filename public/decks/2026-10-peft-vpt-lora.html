Two papers on parameter-efficient fine-tuning today: first VPT, then LoRA, then my own runs of both. The picture is the whole method: the blue Transformer stays frozen, the image goes in as patches, and only the few red vectors in front of them and the red head on top are trained. VPT is the vision paper that took "prompt tuning" from language models and asked whether it works on image encoders. For supervised ViTs it works surprisingly well, and at the end I will run the models I trained, live, in this browser. About seventy minutes in all; please interrupt.

Full fine-tuning stores a new copy of the model per task

Full fine-tuning retrains every weight of the pretrained model on the new task, and it is usually the most accurate option. The cost is that each task keeps its own retrained copy, so storage grows with every task and with the size of the model.

Cheaper methods existed, but none matched full fine-tuning

In 2020, a prompt was text. GPT-3, a frozen model with 175 billion weights, was only trained to continue text, yet it does a new task when you type a description of the task in front of the input. Not a single weight changes. But words are discrete, so the only way to improve a prompt is trial and error.

In 2021, prompt tuning (Lester et al.) replaced the words with learned vectors that correspond to no word. They are trained by gradient descent on the task's examples while the model stays frozen, and they are all you store per task. Prefix-tuning (Li & Liang) does the same at every layer.

The sentiment example is illustrative. Brown et al. 2020 (GPT-3); Lester, Al-Rfou, Constant 2021 (prompt tuning, T5); Li & Liang 2021 (prefix-tuning).

Two steps, one idea. In 2020, GPT-3 showed that a frozen language model does new tasks if you type a description of the task in front of the input; that text is the prompt, and no weight changes. Its weakness: words are discrete, so you can only improve a prompt by trial and error. In 2021, prompt tuning kept the frozen model and replaced the words with vectors trained by gradient descent: the red squares correspond to no word at all. Per task you store k vectors of 1,024 numbers, roughly 20,000 numbers instead of billions. Prefix-tuning does the same at every layer. LoRA, the second paper in this talk, also dates from 2021 but takes a different route: it changes the weights, not the input.

How a Vision Transformer reads an image

A fixed grid cuts the image into 196 patches of 16 × 16 pixels, and each patch, read row by row, becomes a vector of 768 numbers. One extra vector, [CLS], goes in front to collect a summary of the whole image: the 12 layers mix all 197 vectors, and the head reads only [CLS] to name the class.

The layers only see a sequence of vectors, so we can add a few more.

ViT-B/16: 12 layers, vectors of 768 numbers, 85.8 million weights. Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021. Image: a melanoma from DermaMNIST, 224 × 224 pixels.

Spend a minute here, it pays off. Walk the picture from left to right and up: a fixed grid cuts the image into 14 by 14 patches, whatever the image shows; nothing is selected, all 196 go in, read row by row from patch 1 at the top left to 196 at the bottom right. Each patch, 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 pixel values, goes through one linear layer to become a grey vector of 768 numbers (the two 768s are a coincidence of ViT-B), with a position vector added so the model knows where it came from. In front sits one extra learned vector, [CLS], which carries no image content and collects a summary as it goes up. The 12 Transformer layers, blue because they will stay frozen in VPT, let every vector look at every other one. At the top, only the [CLS] vector goes to the head, the one task-specific part, which outputs melanoma. The point for VPT: the Transformer does not care how long the sequence is or where a vector came from, so we can add our own vectors.

The grey vectors come from the input, as before. The red ones do not: they start as random numbers, are trained on the task's examples while the model stays frozen, and are then put in front of every input of that task. A visual prompt is therefore not text and not a pattern painted on the pixels, only learned numbers in the model's input space.

In language, prompt tuning matched full fine-tuning only once the model had about 10 billion weights. VPT beats full fine-tuning on 20 of 24 tasks with an 86-million-weight ViT-B.

Lester et al. 2021 (T5); Jia et al. 2022, Table 1. Not to be confused with CoOp (Zhou et al. 2021, text prompts for CLIP) or Bahng et al. 2022 (a pixel pattern).

The one-sentence version: VPT is prompt tuning for a Vision Transformer. Both rows learn vectors in the model's input space and leave the model alone; the only difference is where the other vectors come from, words or image patches. Say plainly what it is not: not the text prompts for CLIP that CoOp learns, and not a learned pattern painted on the pixels as in Bahng et al.; the paper tests the pixel version too and it is clearly weaker. Then the surprise: in language, learned prompts needed about 10 billion weights to match full fine-tuning; VPT beats it on 20 of 24 tasks with 86 million. The authors themselves suggest vision prompts may behave differently from language prompts. Point at the labels under each row: the grey vectors come from the input, the red ones never do; they start random, are trained on the task, and the same set goes in front of every image of that task.

VPT trains a few extra input vectors and the head

Figures: Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 2. Equations (6) and (8) of the paper: x class token, E patch vectors, P prompts, Z the prompt positions after a layer, Li layer i.

Shallow first: p red vectors are put in front of the patch vectors once, before layer 1. They then travel through all 12 frozen layers like any other token; the paper calls their outputs Z. Only P and the head are trained, about 0.14% of the model for 50 prompts and a 102-class head. Press the arrow key for deep: every layer gets its own fresh set of p vectors, and whatever came out of the previous layer at those positions is thrown away, the blank in the equation. Twelve times the parameters, still only half a percent of the model, and deep is the variant that beats full fine-tuning. Position does not matter because prompts are added after the position embedding.

Why a few extra tokens can steer a frozen network

In every layer, each token takes in information from all the other tokens; this is attention. With prompts in the sequence, [CLS] and every patch also take in information from the prompts.

So training the prompts changes what the frozen network computes, without changing a single weight.

Mechanism: Sec. 3 of Jia et al. 2022; Appendix B relates it to prefix-tuning ("VPT-prefix"). Line widths and the red share of [CLS] are illustrative, not measured.

The intuition people need: a frozen function can still be steered if you control part of its input. Start without prompts: inside a frozen layer, the [CLS] token collects a mix of the patch tokens. Press the arrow key: the red prompts join the sequence, and now part of what [CLS] collects comes from them. Every other token sees the prompts too, at every layer. No weight changes, and nothing is read from the prompt outputs themselves; in VPT-deep they are discarded after each layer. If asked: the paper points out this is equivalent to prepending learned keys and values inside attention, which is what prefix-tuning does in NLP.

The prompts start random, with Xavier-uniform values as in the official code. The paper tried smarter starts, such as class prototypes, and random did as well or better.

Training is plain SGD with momentum for 100 epochs on a cosine schedule. Learning rate and weight decay are picked on a validation split, for every method alike.

The one new hyperparameter is the prompt length p, chosen per task from {1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200} on the validation split.

Recipe: Jia et al. 2022, Sec. 4.1 and Appendix A. Code is my re-implementation, which reproduces the paper's Oxford Flowers result (99.1% vs the paper's 99.0%).

The code is there to make it concrete, not to read line by line. The red lines are the whole method: a prompt tensor of 12 × p × 768 numbers, a head, and three lines inside the layer loop. The first of those slices away what the previous layer produced at the prompt positions, then this layer's fresh prompts go in next to [CLS] and the patches, and the frozen layer runs as usual. Underneath: random initialisation is fine, the optimiser is standard SGD, and the only new knob is p, picked on validation.

Four places to put the prompt

Numbers from Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 5 (ViT-B/16, prompts in the first layer only); "All four" is the mean of the three groups. Diagrams redrawn.

The question: where should the learned numbers enter the network? Two places in principle: in the embedding space, as vectors next to the patch vectors, or in pixel space, as part of the image before the embedding layer. Prepend is the default we have seen: extra tokens after the embedding. Add: the prompts are added onto patch vectors, so the sequence is not longer; it loses in all three groups, 8 points on Structured, and the deep version loses too (43.2 against 55.0 on Structured). Prepend-pixel: learned pixel patches next to the image patches, then the frozen embedding layer; loses everywhere, 5 points on Natural. Concat-channel: a learned fourth channel on the image, the embedding layer trained to read it; it collapses, 30 points lower on Natural and below even a linear probe there (68.9). Summary: separate tokens in the embedding space are the right place. All numbers are VPT-shallow because that is the only setting where the paper tests all four options.

Where VPT falls short

Be honest here; it is what a reading group is for. Three settings where the main result does not carry over. First, the pretraining. Everything so far used a ViT-B pretrained with labels on ImageNet-21k. The paper repeats VTAB-1k with the same architecture pretrained without labels on ImageNet-1k, by MoCo v3 (contrastive) and by MAE (masked autoencoder). The chart shows the mean of the three VTAB group averages. With labels, VPT-deep leads full fine-tuning, 72.0 against 69.0. With MoCo v3 it is 65.2 against 69.6, and Bias (69.2), Partial-1 (68.3) and the adapter (68.2) are all ahead of it. With MAE it collapses to 41.1, closer to the linear probe at 32.1 than to full fine-tuning at 64.3, while Partial-1, which retrains only the last block, reaches 61.5. The authors only say these self-supervised ViTs are fundamentally different and leave the reason open. It connects to the MAE paper from last time: MAE features are known to probe badly, and a few input tokens apparently cannot reorganise them the way retraining the last block can. Press the arrow key for Swin, a hierarchical Transformer that attends within local windows and merges patches in deeper layers; the prompts sit in the windows and are dropped at patch merging. VPT still beats the other cheap methods (Bias, linear probe, Partial, MLP-3) in every group, but full fine-tuning is ahead of VPT-deep in all three, by 6.3 points on Structured. Oddly, VPT-shallow does slightly better than full fine-tuning on Natural, 79.9 against 79.1; it is left out of the chart to keep it simple. Press again for segmentation: ADE20K, 150 classes per pixel, SETR on a ViT-L pretrained on ImageNet-21k; mIoU is the mean overlap between predicted and true regions. The decoder head is trained in every method, which is why even "head only" trains 13 million weights. VPT-deep gets 42.1 against 48.3, a little below Bias at 43.4; prompts plus biases give 44.0, within 1.5 of a fully fine-tuned ResNet-101 with four times the trained weights. This is also where LoRA will come in: it edits weights, so it does not depend on the input space being "steerable".

VPT saves storage, not training memory or inference time

Easy to miss in the main paper: the saving is disk, not GPU. Three arrow-key steps, same rows each time: full fine-tuning in dark with the dashed line at its value, the cheap baselines in grey, VPT-deep in red with 1, 50, 100 and 200 prompts per layer. Storage: a task keeps only what it trained, so VPT-deep is 0.35 MB with one prompt per layer and 7.7 MB with 200, against 343 MB for a full copy; bias and adapter are as small, so storage is what all these methods buy, and it is VPT's real win. Training memory: no saving. The prompts in the first layer only get a gradient if it travels back through all twelve frozen layers, so their activations are kept, as in full fine-tuning; with 200 prompts the longer sequence makes it 25.8 GB against 11.7. Only the linear probe, 0.9 GB, avoids this. Training time per image is not shown: one prompt trains faster than full fine-tuning, 214 against 359 ms, but 200 prompts are as slow, 360 ms. Inference time: every test image carries the prompts through all twelve layers. Fifty prompts take the sequence from 197 to 247 tokens and the time from 70 to 82 ms; 200 prompts double it, 141 ms. The paper notes this is far from the 4x one would expect from quadratic attention. The prompts cannot be folded into the weights, so the cost stays; the paper's prefix variant, which puts the prompts only into the keys and values, recovers some of it (Fig. 19). LoRA, the second paper, merges its update into the weights and costs nothing at test time. If asked: the table has only 1 and 200 prompts; the 50 and 100 values are read off Fig. 18's plotted points, and the 1 and 200 points of that figure match the table. If someone asks "why not just use this for everything", this is the answer.

Every fine-tuned copy is as big as the model

The paper starts from the largest model of its time, GPT-3, with 175 billion weights. Each fine-tuned copy takes 350 GB, so a service with a hundred tasks would store 35 TB. LoRA keeps one shared model and only a small file per task, and the frozen weights also need no optimizer state, which cuts training memory from 1.2 TB to 350 GB.

Earlier cheap methods cost time or sequence length

Adapters insert small extra layers into every block. They have few weights, but an image or a sentence must pass through them one after another, so serving a single input takes 20 to 30% longer. With large batches the gap shrinks to 2 or 3%.

Prompt methods such as prefix tuning and VPT lengthen the input instead. That leaves less room for the real input and costs compute in every layer, and on GPT-3 prefix tuning got worse beyond 256 prompt tokens.

LoRA adds no layer and no token at test time.

Hu et al. 2022, Table 1: one forward pass of GPT-2 medium, mean of 100 trials on one NVIDIA Quadro RTX 8000; bars are drawn relative to the fine-tuned model in each setting. Prefix tuning: Sec. 5.5 and Table 15.

Section 3 of the paper, "aren't existing solutions good enough?". Two families existed. Adapters, the small bottleneck networks of Houlsby et al., add depth: GPU latency depends on how many layers run one after another, so even a tiny adapter adds time when there is only one input to process, the online case. Measured on GPT-2 medium with one input of 128 tokens: 19.8 ms for the plain or fine-tuned model, 23.9 ms with one adapter per block, 25.8 ms with two. With large batches the overhead almost disappears, 2–3%. The second family is prompts, which we know from VPT: they reserve part of the sequence and every layer has to process them; the paper also found them hard to optimise. LoRA's design goal is to avoid both: nothing extra at test time.

What a low-rank matrix is

A grayscale photo is a matrix of 274 × 274 brightness values. Any matrix can be written as a sum of simple pieces, where each piece is one column of numbers times one row. The rank is the number of pieces.

LoRA bets that the change fine-tuning makes to a weight matrix is like this photo: a few pieces carry almost all of it.

Not from the paper: the best rank-r approximation (SVD) of an Oxford Pets photo, Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 10. The low-rank bet follows Li et al. 2018 and Aghajanyan et al. 2021.

One minute of linear algebra, because the whole paper rests on the word rank. Treat the photo as a matrix of brightness values. A rank-one matrix is a column times a row: every row of the image is the same pattern, just scaled, so you get bands. Step through with the arrow keys. Each step doubles the number of pieces. The pieces are ordered by how much they contribute, so the first few capture the big shapes and the later ones only fine detail. At rank 16 the cat is recognisable, from 12% of the numbers; at 64 it is hard to tell from the original, from 47%. Storing r pieces costs r times (rows plus columns) numbers instead of rows times columns. The drawing under the text shows the same thing as matrices, to scale: the r columns side by side are a tall matrix B, the r rows stacked are a wide matrix A, and B times A rebuilds the photo; at rank 1 they are a single line each, at all 274 they are as big as the photo itself. That is the trade LoRA makes for the weight change, with the same names B and A from now on. Why expect the change to be low-rank? Two earlier papers measured the "intrinsic dimension" of fine-tuning: you can freeze the model, train only a few hundred or thousand numbers, map them through a fixed random projection onto all the weights, and still get most of the accuracy. LoRA's assumption is the matrix version of that: each weight change has a low rank, so it can be written as B times A with a small r, chosen by hand.

LoRA adds a trainable bypass to a frozen weight

Figure: Hu et al. 2022, Fig. 1, where orange is trained. Equation (3) and Sec. 4.1. The paper sets α to the first r it tries and does not tune it.

The paper's own figure; orange there is what we call red, the trained part. During training: x goes through the frozen W0 as before, and in parallel through A, which squeezes it down to r numbers, and B, which expands it back. The outputs are added. Two details matter. B starts at zero, so at step one the model is exactly the pretrained model and training starts from a known good point. And the bypass is scaled by alpha over r, so that changing the rank does not mean re-tuning the learning rate. Press the arrow key: after training, B times A is just a d × d matrix, so we add it into W0 once and throw A and B away. The model is again a single matrix per layer, same size, same speed. To switch task you subtract this task's BA and add another task's, a cheap operation. That is the property VPT did not have.

LoRA on a 3 × 3 weight matrix

Made-up numbers with rank r = 1 and α/r = 1, so every product can be checked by eye. In ViT-B the same happens with 768 × 768 weights and r = 8.

Made-up numbers, rank 1, so every product can be checked by eye. First the frozen path: the pretrained layer computes W times x, which gives 5, 7 and 8. Next step: LoRA adds a second path. A is one row of three numbers, so A times x is a single number, 1 + 2 + 0 = 3; the input is squeezed down to r numbers. B is a column of three, so B times 3 gives 3, 0 and 6, back to full size. The two paths are added: 8, 7, 14. During training only these six red numbers would change. Next step: after training we multiply B by A once. A column times a row is a full 3 × 3 matrix, the same shape as W, so we can add it to W; the red cells are the ones that changed. Last step: the merged W′ times x gives 8, 7, 14 again, exactly the training-time output, because W x + B(A x) equals (W + BA) x. So A and B are deleted and the layer is one ordinary matrix. The point people often miss: B and A are never multiplied with W. They are multiplied with each other, and the product is added to W.

VPT and LoRA side by side

One layer of ViT-B/16 drawn for each. VPT-deep with 50 prompts per layer (Jia et al. 2022); LoRA with rank 8 on the query and value weights (Hu et al. 2022). Both train the same 102-class head.

This is the slide that puts the two methods next to each other, in three steps. What is learned: VPT learns extra input vectors, 50 per layer, and the same ones are used for every image of the task; they start random, they are not cut from any image. LoRA learns a correction to the weights, the pair B and A beside the query and value weights; it is also one pair for the whole task, not per image, and B starts at zero. Next step, training: in both cases every training image goes through the frozen model, the loss is computed, and gradients flow back. In VPT they end in the prompts and the head; in LoRA in B, A and the head. In both, the pretrained weights never change. Next step, test time, which is where the two differ. VPT's prompts are inputs, so they have to be attached to every test image, and every layer processes 247 tokens instead of 197, 27% more compute. LoRA's correction is a matrix of the same shape as W, so it is added into W once, and the layer is an ordinary layer again, exactly the original cost. The next slide puts numbers on both differences: what each stores per task, and what each image costs.

A full change to one d × d weight has d² numbers, while B and A together have 2rd. Since d is the square root of d², LoRA's cost grows like the square root of the full size, times the small constant 2r.

So the wider the model, the smaller LoRA's share. Together with adapting only the query and value weights, this is why GPT-3's task file is 10,000 times smaller than a copy.

One d × d attention weight, rank r = 8 for every model. Widths: ViT-B/16 768, ViT-L/16 1,024, ViT-H/14 1,280 (Dosovitskiy et al. 2021); GPT-3 12,288 (Hu et al. 2022, Sec. 1). 10,000 times: Hu et al. 2022, Sec. 4.2.

One more way to see the saving. A full change to a d by d weight has d squared numbers. B is d by r and A is r by d, so together 2 r d. Since d is the square root of d squared, LoRA's cost grows like the square root of the full size, multiplied by 2r, and r is a small constant we choose, 1 to 16. The chart keeps the rank fixed at 8 and only changes the width of the model. On ViT-B, 768 wide, the pair is 2.1% of a full change. ViT-H, 1,280 wide, 1.25%. GPT-3 is 12,288 wide, so the same rank is 0.13% of each weight. Add that GPT-3 only gets pairs on two of its weight types per block, and you arrive at the paper's headline: a 35 MB file instead of a 350 GB copy, 10,000 times smaller. For our ViT-B the saving is real but much less dramatic, which is why the file is 1.5 MB and not a few KB.

LoRA on a ViT in a few lines

vit = timm.create_model(BACKBONE, pretrained=True, num_classes=C)vit.requires_grad_(False); vit.head.requires_grad_(True) class LoRAqv(nn.Module):              # wraps qkv = Linear(768, 2304)    def __init__(self, qkv, r=8, alpha=8):        super().__init__()        d = qkv.in_features        self.qkv, self.s = qkv, alpha / r    # frozen W0, scale α/r        self.Aq = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, d) / d ** 0.5)        self.Av = nn.Parameter(torch.randn(r, d) / d ** 0.5)        self.Bq = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d, r))   # B = 0        self.Bv = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(d, r))    def forward(self, x):        q, k, v = self.qkv(x).chunk(3, dim=-1)      # W0 x        q = q + self.s * x @ self.Aq.T @ self.Bq.T  # + B A x        v = v + self.s * x @ self.Av.T @ self.Bv.T        return torch.cat([q, k, v], dim=-1) for blk in vit.blocks: blk.attn.qkv = LoRAqv(blk.attn.qkv)

B starts at zero and A at small random values, so the first forward pass is exactly the pretrained model.

The two new choices are the rank r and which weights get a pair. On ViT-B, Zhang et al. use rank 8 with AdamW at a learning rate of 0.001 for 100 epochs.

After training, each pair is added into its qkv weight once, and the model is a plain timm ViT again.

My sketch for timm's ViT-B/16: 294,912 trained numbers in the pairs, and identical outputs before and after merging. Official code: loralib, github.com/microsoft/LoRA. Settings: Hu et al. 2022, Sec. 4.1; Zhang et al. 2022, App. A.2.

Not to be read line by line. timm stores query, key and value as one fused linear layer of 768 to 2304, so the wrapper splits its output into three, adds the low-rank term to the query and value parts, and puts them back. The red lines are the whole method: four small matrices per block and two additions. The blue lines are frozen. I checked it on timm's ViT-B/16: it has exactly the 294,912 trained numbers from the counting slide, and after folding B times A into the qkv weight the outputs are identical up to rounding. The recipe on ViT from the NOAH paper is ordinary: AdamW, learning rate 1e-3, cosine schedule, 100 epochs, rank 8.

Which weight matrices to adapt

Hu et al. 2022, Table 5: GPT-3 175B with the same 18 million trained weights in every LoRA row, validation accuracy (noise ±0.5 on WikiSQL, ±0.1 on MNLI). Full fine-tuning: Table 4.

A fixed budget of 18 million trained weights, 35 MB, spent in different ways. One weight type at rank 8: the query or the key alone is clearly worse, about three points on WikiSQL. Two types at rank 4: query and value is the best combination. All four at rank 2 is as good. The conclusion the authors draw: it is better to adapt more weight matrices with a smaller rank than one matrix with a large rank, which is also a hint that rank 4 already holds what each matrix needs. This is why query and value became the default, including on ViT.

How small the rank can be

Hu et al. 2022, Table 6: GPT-3 175B, validation accuracy at five ranks; noise ±0.5 on WikiSQL and ±0.1 on MNLI. On GPT-2 the best rank was between 4 and 16 (App. H.2).

Five ranks, one dot each; the lines only connect the settings that were run. The surprise of the paper: for query and value together, rank 1 is as good as rank 64 on both tasks; the spread across ranks is at most 0.4 points on WikiSQL, inside its noise, and 0.3 on MNLI. The query weight alone is the exception, it needs rank 4 or more on WikiSQL. The authors are careful: they do not expect a tiny rank to work for every task. If the new task were in a different language than pretraining, retraining everything would likely win. On the smaller GPT-2 the best rank was 4 to 16. For ViT, NOAH used rank 8, which is the setting we will see.

Where LoRA falls short

The rank and the weights are chosen by hand

The paper picks the query and value weights and a small rank by trying settings, and leaves a principled choice to future work. On VTAB-1k the best method even changes from task to task.

Merged weights serve one task at a time

Once BA is added into W, every input in a batch must belong to that task. Mixing tasks in one batch means keeping the pairs separate, which brings back a small extra cost; VPT's prompts mix freely.

A small rank assumes a nearby task

The authors expect a small rank to fail when the new task is far from pretraining, such as a different language. The low-rank analysis was only done on GPT-3, not on vision models.

Training still runs through the whole model

LoRA saves the optimizer state of the frozen weights, but the gradient still passes back through every layer. On ViT-B most memory is activations: freezing the backbone for VPT only cut it from 11.7 to 10.3 GB.

Hu et al. 2022, Sec. 4.2 (batching), footnote 6 (a different language), Sec. 7 and 8 (analysis on GPT-3, future work). Zhang et al. 2022, Fig. 1b. Jia et al. 2022, Table 12 (ViT-B/16, batch 64).

Four honest limits. First, choices by hand: which matrices, which rank. The paper's guidance comes from GPT-3, and NOAH's per-task spread shows the best choice varies. Second, a deployment detail people miss: merging is what makes LoRA free at test time, but a merged model serves one task. If a server mixes requests of different tasks in one batch, you have to keep the pairs unmerged and pay a small extra computation, while VPT's prompts are just inputs and can differ per sample. Third, the low-rank assumption: the authors themselves say a very different task, like a new language, may need full rank; the evidence for low rank is from GPT-3 only. Fourth, training memory: the 3× saving on GPT-3 came from optimizer state, which dominates for huge models. On ViT-B, activations dominate, and those are still stored for the whole network, as with VPT. My own measurement of this will be part of the experiments next.

What each method costs on skin lesions

Three steps, one cost each, all measured on my laptop for skin lesions trained on the full training split; every number is read from the training logs and the benchmark file. Storage: both LoRA and VPT keep one copy of the base model and add a small file per task, while full fine-tuning needs a whole 343 MB model per task. Training: LoRA and VPT both still send the error signal back through all twelve frozen layers, so neither is cheap like the linear probe; the memory saving compared with full fine-tuning is the optimizer state of the frozen weights. Inference: this is where the two differ. VPT makes every layer process 50 extra tokens. LoRA with its pairs kept separate adds a little extra work, and once merged into the weights it runs exactly as fast as the plain model, which is the paper's main practical claim.
