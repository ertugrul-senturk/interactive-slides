The task: render a scene from a viewpoint nobody photographed

NeRFone network, trained on these photos only

The input is a set of ordinary photos of one scene, plus where each camera stood and where it pointed. The output is the photo a camera would take from any other position, with correct perspective, hidden parts and reflections.

This problem is called view synthesis. NeRF solves it by training one network per scene, from scratch, on that scene's photos alone. After training, the network is the scene.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Fig. 1: the synthetic Drums scene, 100 input views on a hemisphere, two rendered new views.

Start with the task, not the method. We have photos of one scene, and we know for every photo the camera's position and direction; for real photos these come from a structure-from-motion tool, COLMAP, which I will use myself at the end. We want the photo from a new viewpoint. Note what is not here: no 3D scanner, no depth sensor, no pretrained model, no big dataset. One scene, about 100 photos, one network trained only on them. That last sentence is the key mental shift: the network is not a model that understands many scenes; it is a compressed description of this single scene.

Storing a scene: a grid of cubes or a function

A slice through a toy scene, drawn with the grid chosen below.

Earlier methods store a scene as a 3D grid of small cubes, called voxels, each with a colour and an opacity. Fine detail needs small cubes, and halving their size multiplies their number by eight.

NeRF stores a function instead: a network that gives the colour and density at any point you ask about. Its size does not depend on a resolution, and for a whole scene it is about 5 MB.

Density says how much matter is at the point: zero in empty air, small in something see-through, large in a solid. It does not depend on where you look from.

Colour is the light the point sends towards the camera, so it can change with the viewing direction.

A toy scene in two dimensions, with made-up density values; NeRF does the same in 3D. Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 3: FΘ : (x, d) → (c, σ).

This is the function NeRF learns, five numbers in and four out: a 3D position plus a viewing direction in, a density and an RGB colour out. The toy scene is seen from above: an orange pane you can partly see through, and a solid blue ball behind it. Step through three points with the arrow key, or click anywhere. In empty air the density is zero, and then the colour does not matter because nothing is there. Inside the pane the density is small: light is dimmed but passes. On the ball it is high: opaque. Density depends only on the position, colour also on the direction; that split comes back on the network slide.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Eq. 3; δi is the gap between neighbouring points. Toy scene and density values as on the previous slide, 64 points per ray; the rendered strip is every pixel of the camera, computed with the same sum.

This is the core of the paper, so take it slowly. Step 0: each pixel looks along one ray; NeRF asks the network at 64 points along it, spread evenly with a little random jitter so that, over training, every position gets queried. Step 1: the density at each point; zero in air, a bit in the pane, high in the ball. Step 2: T, the share of light from that point that still reaches the camera. It starts at 1 and drops every time the ray passes through matter. This is Beer–Lambert, the same law as X-ray attenuation in CT, where a ray is dimmed by the integral of the attenuation along it. Step 3: each point's contribution is T times its opacity, and the pixel is the sum of contributions times colours. The front surface wins, what is behind it hardly counts. Drag the slider to choose another pixel: through the pane only you get a pale orange, through both a dark blue tinted by the pane, past everything the white background. The strip on the left is the whole rendered image row. Everything here is differentiable, which is what makes training possible.

Training: render pixels we know and compare

Each training step picks 4,096 random pixels from all the input photos. Every pixel is rendered with the current network, exactly as on the previous slide, and compared with its true colour.

loss = Σrays ‖ Crendered − Cphoto ‖²

The error flows back through the rendering sum into the network's weights. Nothing tells the network where the surfaces are: a point gets high density only because that makes the renders match the photos from many cameras at once.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Fig. 2 and Eq. 6 (the loss is summed over the coarse and fine renders, introduced later). Batch of 4,096 rays: Sec. 5.3.

Walk the figure from left to right. (a) Rays from two of the training cameras, with points along them. (b) Every point goes through the network, which returns colour and density. (c) Along each ray, the densities and colours are composited into one colour, the rendering sum we just saw. (d) Compare with the true pixel and take the squared error. Then backpropagate through all of it. The important part is the right paragraph: there is no 3D supervision at all. If the network put density in the wrong place, the pixel would be right for one camera but wrong for the others. The only way to match all photos at once is to put the matter where it really is, so the 3D shape appears by itself.

The network sees the direction only at the very end

The position passes through eight layers before the network outputs the density. Because the direction is not used there, the shape is the same from every viewpoint, which keeps the views consistent.

The direction joins only for the last layer, enough to tint the colour for reflections and shine. Each network has about 0.6 million weights; with the second network introduced later, a scene takes 5 MB.

Redrawn from Mildenhall et al. 2020, Fig. 7 and Sec. 3. γ is the positional encoding (60 numbers for the position, 24 for the direction), explained shortly. Weight count computed from the layer sizes; 5 MB is from Sec. 6.3.

This is the paper's Fig. 7, redrawn. Grey are inputs, red are the trained layers, dark outlines the outputs. Eight plain fully connected layers with ReLU and 256 units each; the encoded position is fed in again at the fifth layer, a trick borrowed from DeepSDF. Then the density comes out, and a 256-number feature that is joined with the viewing direction for one last layer of 128 units, which gives RGB. So density is a function of position only, colour of position and direction. That design choice is deliberate: if density could depend on direction, the network could cheat by inventing different geometry for every camera. Counting the layer sizes gives about 594,000 weights per network; two networks at 32 bits is the paper's 5 MB.

The same point can look different from different sides

The figure follows two fixed points of the Ship scene, one on the hull and one on the water. Seen from two different cameras, each point has a different colour, because the surfaces reflect light.

The discs on the right show the colour the network predicts for that point from every direction of the hemisphere. A model that ignores the direction has to average these into one colour.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Fig. 3: (a) view 1, (b) view 2, (c) predicted colour over all viewing directions for the hull point (orange ring) and the water point (blue ring).

This is why the direction input is there. Pick the hull point marked with the diamond: from view 1 it is a dull red, from view 2, marked with the square, it is bright because of a specular highlight. The disc shows the whole function: for that one 3D point, the colour for every viewing direction over the hemisphere. Same for the water. Surfaces like these, whose colour depends on the angle, are called non-Lambertian; most earlier neural methods assumed the opposite. The ablation later puts a number on it: without direction, the score drops by 3.4 dB.

Why raw coordinates give blurry results

A network fed raw coordinates learns smooth functions first and sharp edges very slowly. Here a small network is trained live, in this browser, to reproduce one row of pixels from the Lego image.

Feeding it sines and cosines of the position at ten frequencies, instead of the position itself, lets it fit edges as sharp as the image.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 5.1, Eq. 4; NeRF uses L = 10 for the position (60 numbers) and L = 4 for the direction (24). Demo: 256 pixels of row 440 of the Lego test image, a 3-layer network with 64 units, 600 Adam steps, same seed for both inputs. PSNR measures the fit to these pixels.

This is a live experiment, not an animation: a three-layer network with 64 units is trained right now, in the browser, to map a pixel's position along the row to its colour. Raw position: after 600 steps it is still a blur, around 18 dB. Press the arrow key for the encoded input: the same network, same seed, same steps, but the position goes in as sines and cosines at ten frequencies, and it reproduces the row almost exactly, above 40 dB. The reason is known as spectral bias: networks fit low frequencies first. The encoding hands the network high-frequency building blocks for free. Do not confuse it with the Transformer's positional encoding; the formula is similar, the purpose is different. NeRF applies it to x, y and z separately with ten frequencies each, 60 numbers, and to the direction with four, 24 numbers.

A coarse pass decides where to look closely

64 coarse points, evenly spread128 fine points

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 5.2, Eq. 5: Nc = 64 coarse and Nf = 128 fine points, drawn by inverse transform sampling from the coarse weights. Toy scene as before.

Most of a ray is empty air or hidden behind the first surface, and evaluating the network there is wasted work. So NeRF trains two networks. Step 0: a coarse network is evaluated at 64 evenly spread points and the ray is rendered as before. Step 1: the contributions w of those 64 points, the same w as in the rendering sum, show where the colour came from: the pane and the front of the ball. Step 2: they are normalised into a probability distribution along the ray and 128 more points are drawn from it, so they land where the matter is. A second, fine network is evaluated at all 192 points and gives the final colour. Both networks are trained with the same loss, so the coarse one keeps getting better at pointing the way. This is classic importance sampling from graphics, Levoy 1990.

For real photos, the camera positions come from COLMAP, a structure-from-motion program that matches features across the photos themselves.

Put it all together in one step. Sample 4,096 random pixels from all photos at once. For each ray, 64 coarse points, render with the coarse network and keep the weights; draw 128 more points where the weights are large; render again with the fine network at all 192 points. The loss is the squared error of both renders against the true pixel colours. Adam, with the learning rate decaying exponentially. Then the cost, which matters later: a day or two of training per scene on a V100, and 30 seconds to render one image, because every pixel is about 200 network calls. For real photos the poses come from COLMAP, the same tool I used on my own capture.

The three test sets

Four simple objects with matte surfaces, from DeepVoxels. 479 training views at 512 × 512.

Eight complex objects with shiny and see-through materials, made for this paper. 100 training views at 800 × 800, 200 test views.

Eight real scenes shot with a phone, all from the front. 20 to 62 photos each, one in eight held out for testing.

Every method renders the held-out views, and each render is compared with the real image. PSNR is the pixel error in decibels, and 3 dB more means half the squared error. SSIM compares local structure (1 is perfect). LPIPS compares the images through a deep network, closer to human judgement; lower is better.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 6.1 and Table 1. Images are test views from Fig. 5 and Fig. 6. DeepVoxels: Sitzmann et al. 2019; LPIPS: Zhang et al. 2018; SSIM: Wang et al. 2004.

Three test sets, from easy to hard. The first is DeepVoxels' four matte objects; the paper shows no images of them. The second is new in this paper: eight path-traced objects with complicated geometry and non-Lambertian materials, Lego, Ship, Drums, Mic and so on, 100 training views each. The third is real: eight forward-facing phone captures, five from the LLFF paper and three new, with 20 to 62 photos. Quality is measured on held-out views. The one number to have a feel for is PSNR: it is a log scale of the mean squared error, so +3 dB halves the error and +10 dB divides it by ten. SSIM and LPIPS are there because PSNR does not match human perception well; LPIPS in particular punishes blur.

What a decibel of PSNR means

A decibel measures how many times larger one power is than another: ten times the logarithm of their ratio. That is why it is used for sound, radio signals and images alike.

PSNR compares the brightest possible pixel with the average squared error between the render and the photo. Every 10 dB means ten times less squared error; 3 dB means half.

PSNR = 10 log10(1 / mean squared error) for pixel values from 0 to 1. Image: NeRF's Lego render from Mildenhall et al. 2020, with random noise added until its PSNR against the clean image is the value shown.

A short detour because the unit looks odd. A decibel is not a unit of sound. It is ten times the base-ten logarithm of a ratio of two powers; Bell Labs introduced it for losses on telephone lines, and sound pressure, Wi-Fi signal strength and amplifier gain all use it. In PSNR the two powers are the largest possible pixel value squared, which is 1, and the mean squared error. So 26 dB means the error is 10 to the minus 2.6, a four-hundredth. The steps show what that looks like: noise was added to the Lego image until its PSNR is exactly 20, 26, 31 and 40 dB. 26 is about Neural Volumes on the synthetic scenes, 31 is NeRF there, 40 is NeRF on the easy matte objects. The typical error per pixel is 255 times 10 to the minus PSNR over 20 brightness levels. One caution: NeRF's errors look like blur, not noise, and PSNR does not distinguish the two; that is why SSIM and LPIPS are reported as well.

Small to store, slow to train and to render

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 6.3 (storage, training time) and Appendix A (30 s per 800 × 800 image on a V100). Squares are sized by area.

The honest trade-off, in two steps. Storage: LLFF keeps a layered image for every input view, over 15 GB for one synthetic scene; NeRF's two networks are 5 MB, three thousand times less, and even less than the input photos. Time: LLFF processes a new scene in under ten minutes because its network was trained in advance on many scenes; every method that fits one scene from scratch needs at least 12 hours, and NeRF one to two days. Rendering is slow too, about 30 seconds per image. This is exactly what the next two papers attack.

Making it fast

NeRF needs one to two days to train a scene and half a minute to render one image. Two later papers removed most of that cost.

Instant-NGP (2022) keeps the rays but moves most of the work from the network into a learned lookup table. 3D Gaussian splatting (2023) drops both the network and the rays.

This is the second part of the talk. NeRF's idea won, but it was slow: days of training per scene and seconds per frame. I picked the two papers that fixed this and that most people use today. Instant-NGP from NVIDIA, SIGGRAPH 2022, keeps NeRF's rendering but makes each query almost free. 3D Gaussian splatting from Inria, SIGGRAPH 2023, keeps NeRF's image formation but throws away the network: the scene is millions of small blobs that a graphics card can draw directly. Both images on the right are from the papers.

Instant-NGP: learned features in a grid, and a tiny network

The paper trains the same scene six ways for 11,000 steps each. NeRF's sine encoding (b) needs a large network of 438,000 weights and takes almost 13 minutes.

A grid of learned feature vectors (c, d) lets a network of 10,000 weights do the rest, so training takes about a minute, but a dense grid needs millions of numbers. A hash table (e, f) keeps the speed and gives the best image, 24.6 dB.

Müller et al. 2022, Fig. 2. Under each image: network weights + encoding parameters, training time (minutes:seconds) and PSNR.

This figure is the whole argument of the paper, so read it from left to right. (a) Raw coordinates: blurry, as we saw. (b) NeRF's sine encoding with NeRF's big network: sharp, but slow, nearly 13 minutes even in their fast code. (c) Instead of making the network do all the work, store learned feature vectors on a 3D grid and interpolate them; then a tiny network of 10,000 weights is enough, and training takes about one minute. The catch is 33.6 million grid numbers, most of them for empty space. (d) Several coarser grids give the same quality with half the numbers. (e, f) The paper's contribution: put the grids in hash tables of fixed size. With a big table it is the best image here, 24.6 dB, at the same speed. The next two slides explain the grids and the hashing.

Looking a point up in grids from coarse to fine

Each of 16 grids, from 16 cells per side to as fine as the photos need, finds the cell around the point (1) and reads the learned vectors stored at its corners (2). They are blended by distance (3), and the 16 results go into a tiny network (4, 5).

A point touches only 8 corners per grid, so each training step changes only a few table entries. The coarse grids describe the overall shape and the fine ones the detail, like the frequencies of NeRF's encoding, but learned.

Müller et al. 2022, Fig. 3 (drawn in 2D, two levels, red and blue) and Table 1: 16 levels, 2 numbers per entry, coarsest grid 16 cells per side.

The figure shows two of the sixteen levels in 2D. The point x falls into a cell of the coarse blue grid and of the finer red grid. Step 1: the corners of these cells get indices; step 2: each index points to an entry in that level's table, which holds two learned numbers; step 3: the four corner values, eight in 3D, are blended according to where x sits in the cell; step 4: the results of all levels are concatenated, together with the encoded viewing direction; step 5: a tiny network maps them to density and colour. Training updates the network and the table entries by backpropagation. The important intuition: the grids play the role of NeRF's frequencies, coarse to fine, but the values are learned and local, so a query is a few memory reads instead of a big matrix multiplication.

Fine grids share one fixed-size table

Illustration with a table of 100 entries; the paper uses up to 219 = 524,288 entries per grid for NeRF. Hash function of the paper (Eq. 4): corner (x, y) goes to entry (x ⊕ 2,654,435,761·y) mod T, where ⊕ is bitwise XOR.

Why hash at all? A fine 3D grid has billions of corners, far too many to store. So every grid gets a table of fixed size T, and corners are mapped into it by a hash function, a cheap formula that scatters them pseudo-randomly. Step through the grid sizes. With 8 by 8 cells there are 81 corners and 100 entries: every corner gets its own entry, no hashing needed; the paper does the same for its coarse grids. At 16 by 16 there are 289 corners, so about three share each entry, and the red rings show the corners that share the entry of our point's corner. At 32 by 32, about eleven share one. The paper does nothing to resolve these collisions. During training, the shared entry gets pulled mostly by the corner that matters, usually one near a visible surface, because empty space produces small gradients; and the network can tell the cases apart using the coarse grids, which never collide.

Why it is so much faster, and what it costs

Computed from the papers: NeRF evaluates 64 + 192 points per ray with a network of about 594,000 weights (Mildenhall et al. 2020, Fig. 7, Sec. 5.3). Instant-NGP uses 3.1 to 25.7 points per ray (Müller et al. 2022, Table 3) and a network of about 10,000 weights (Fig. 2); 12.6 million table entries with T = 219 (Fig. 2f).

Two steps. First, compute per ray. NeRF sends 256 points per ray through a network of roughly 600,000 weights: about 150 million multiply-adds for a single pixel. Instant-NGP keeps a coarse grid that marks which cells are occupied, so it skips empty space and needs only 3 to 26 points per ray, and each point costs 16 small table reads plus a network of 10,000 weights. That is roughly 600 times less arithmetic per ray, and the tiny network fits into the GPU's fastest on-chip memory, which their hand-written CUDA code exploits. Second step, the cost: memory. The tables hold 12.6 million learned numbers, ten times NeRF's 1.2 million weights. It is still a radiance field rendered by ray marching, so rendering is fast but not as fast as rasterisation, which is the next paper.

3D Gaussian splatting: no network, no ray marching

The scene becomes a cloud of a few million small, coloured, see-through blobs called 3D Gaussians. An image is made by projecting every blob onto the screen and blending them, the way a graphics card draws triangles.

On this view, after 6 minutes of training it renders 135 frames per second. After 51 minutes it beats Mip-NeRF 360, which trained for 48 hours and renders one frame every 14 seconds.

Kerbl et al. 2023, Fig. 1: a test view of the Bicycle scene; frame rates and training times on one A6000 GPU. Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al. 2022) was the best-quality NeRF variant at the time; Plenoxels (Fridovich-Keil and Yu et al. 2022) is a sparse voxel grid without a network.

Read the labels under the images. Instant-NGP and Plenoxels train in minutes but render at about 9 frames per second and lose detail. Mip-NeRF 360, the best NeRF of 2022, is the quality reference: 48 hours of training and 0.071 frames per second. Gaussian splatting after 6 minutes is already at Instant-NGP's quality but renders at 135 frames per second; after 51 minutes it beats Mip-NeRF 360's PSNR on this view and still renders at 93 frames per second. The key change: no network to query and no rays to march. The scene is explicit, a list of primitives, and rendering is rasterisation, what GPUs were built for.

What one Gaussian stores

59 numbers per Gaussian, and 1 to 5 million Gaussians per scene.

Kerbl et al. 2023, Sec. 4: the shape is a covariance Σ = R S ST RT, stored as a scale vector and a rotation quaternion; colour as spherical harmonics up to degree 3 (16 per channel).

One Gaussian is a soft ellipsoid, densest at its centre and fading out. Step 0, position and shape: 3 numbers for the centre, 3 sizes along its own axes and a rotation stored as a quaternion of 4 numbers. Stretching lets one Gaussian be a thin needle for a branch or a flat disc for a wall. Step 1: one opacity, how much light it blocks at its centre. Step 2: the colour, which may change with the viewing direction exactly as in NeRF; it is stored as spherical harmonics, a smooth function over directions, 16 coefficients per colour channel, 48 numbers. So most of the memory is colour. 59 numbers per Gaussian, a few million Gaussians, hundreds of megabytes.

Training moves, clones, splits and removes Gaussians

Training starts from the 3D points COLMAP found while computing the camera positions, each one a small round Gaussian. Rendered images are compared with the photos, as in NeRF, and every number of every Gaussian is adjusted by gradient descent.

Every 100 steps the set itself changes. Where detail is missing, a small Gaussian is cloned; where one large Gaussian covers fine detail, it is split in two; nearly transparent ones are removed.

After 30,000 steps a scene has 1 to 5 million Gaussians.

Kerbl et al. 2023, Fig. 4 and Sec. 5. The loss is 0.8 × L1 + 0.2 × D-SSIM between the render and the photo. Cloning and splitting are triggered by large position gradients.

How do a few thousand COLMAP points become millions of well-shaped Gaussians? Everything is differentiable, so position, shape, opacity and colour of every Gaussian are optimised by comparing renders with photos, as in NeRF. In addition, every 100 steps the method looks at Gaussians whose position gradients are large, a sign that the region is not yet explained. If such a Gaussian is small, it is copied and the copy is moved along the gradient: that is under-reconstruction, top row. If it is large, it is split into two smaller ones: over-reconstruction, bottom row. Gaussians whose opacity drops close to zero are deleted. The loss mixes L1 with a structural similarity term. My own capture already has the starting points, as I show at the end.

What Gaussian splatting costs

Every Gaussian keeps 59 numbers and a scene has millions of them: 734 MB on average for the Mip-NeRF 360 scenes, 85 times the size of Mip-NeRF 360's network. Training a large scene can need more than 20 GB of GPU memory.

Where the photos see little, Gaussians become stretched or blotchy, and large ones can pop in and out as the camera moves. The method also needs good COLMAP points to start from.

Kerbl et al. 2023, Table 1 (mean size per scene, Mip-NeRF 360 dataset) and Sec. 7.4 (limitations). Squares are sized by area.

The honest other side. Size: an explicit representation is big, hundreds of megabytes per scene, against a few megabytes for a network; much follow-up work compresses Gaussians. Training memory: the authors report more than 20 GB of GPU memory for large scenes in their prototype. Quality: in regions seen by few photos you get elongated or splotchy Gaussians, and large Gaussians can pop when the sort order changes as the camera moves. And it depends on the structure-from-motion points for its start; random initialisation works on synthetic scenes but is worse on real ones.

Three ways to store the same scene

NeRF, 2020

Two networks with 1.2 million weights, 5 MB. One to two days of training on a V100, and about 30 seconds per image, because every pixel queries the network 256 times.

Instant-NGP, 2022

16 hashed grids with 12.6 million learned entries and a network of 10,000 weights. Seconds to minutes of training on an RTX 3090, still rendered by marching rays.

3D Gaussian splatting, 2023

One to five million Gaussians of 59 numbers each, about 734 MB. About 40 minutes of training on an A6000, and over 100 frames per second.

All three render with the same sum along a ray; they differ in where the density and colour come from.

Numbers from each paper as shown on the previous slides; the GPUs and test scenes differ between papers, so compare orders of magnitude, not exact values.

The one-slide recap of the second part. NeRF: everything in network weights; small file, slow queries. Instant-NGP: most of the information in learned grids, a tiny network; fast queries, more memory, still ray marching. Gaussian splatting: everything explicit, no network; real-time rasterisation, large files. The thread through the whole talk is the callout: all three use NeRF's volume rendering sum. What changed is how expensive each term of that sum is to get.

What else NeRF leaves open

Blur at other distances

One thin ray per pixel misses detail when the camera is closer or further than in training. Mip-NeRF (Barron et al. 2021) traces cones instead, and Mip-NeRF 360 (Barron et al. 2022) extends it to large outdoor scenes.

One network per scene

Nothing is learned across scenes, so each needs many photos. pixelNeRF (Yu et al. 2021) learns a prior from many scenes and works from one or a few photos.

A still scene, fixed light

Moving objects and changing lighting break the assumption that every photo shows the same scene. NeRF in the Wild (Martin-Brualla et al. 2021) handles tourist photos taken at different times.

Medical imaging

CT already measures density along rays. NAF (Zha et al. 2022) keeps only NeRF's density and reconstructs cone-beam CT from few X-ray projections.

Limits from Mildenhall et al. 2020, Sec. 7. Follow-up work as cited; see the references.

Speed was the second part; here are four other limits, with one example each of what came next, without going deep. Aliasing: NeRF samples one infinitely thin ray per pixel, so detail flickers or blurs at distances not seen in training; Mip-NeRF reasons about cones, and Mip-NeRF 360, the quality reference in the Gaussian splatting results, extends it to unbounded scenes. Generalisation: vanilla NeRF learns nothing that transfers, pixelNeRF conditions on image features to work from few views. Static scenes: NeRF-W adds per-photo appearance codes and a transient component. And for this group, medical imaging: the rendering equation is the Beer–Lambert law, so CT is a natural fit; NAF, MICCAI 2022, learns an attenuation field for sparse-view CBCT, density only, no colour.

Keeping only the globe, not my room

Neither NeRF nor Gaussian splatting knows what an object is; they rebuild whatever the photos show. COLMAP still gets the full photos, because the room gives it far more to match. To cut out the globe, SAM 2, a video segmentation model, followed it through all 255 frames from one box and two clicks on the first.

Everything outside the mask is painted white, and NeRF composites its rays onto white. The cheapest way to produce white pixels is empty space, so the room never enters the model.

My capture, frame 1868 of the video. Masks: SAM 2.1 Hiera-L (Ravi et al. 2024) through Hugging Face transformers, prompted with a box and two points in the first frame, then propagated. White background as in the paper's synthetic scenes.

This answers a question I had myself: how does the method know to keep only the globe? It does not. NeRF and Gaussian splatting reconstruct everything the photos show, sofa included. So the separation happens before training, in two steps. First, camera positions: COLMAP runs on the full, unmasked photos, because the furniture, floor and walls give it many more features to match, so the cameras come out more accurate. Second, the mask: SAM 2, Meta's segmentation model for video, needs one box and two clicks, one on the ball and one on the stand, in the first frame, and it follows the object through all 255 frames; the box alone gave me only the stand. Step 1: the photos are composited on white with the soft mask, and NeRF adds white wherever a ray passes through without hitting anything. To make white pixels, NeRF can leave space empty, which is the cheapest solution, so the room disappears. It can also put faint white fog in space; those are the floaters you will see in the depth map on the next slide.

The paper's model and settings: 4,096 rays per step, 64 + 128 samples per ray, 100,000 steps. On my laptop's RTX 5070 Ti this took hours; the paper needed one to two days on a V100 for 100,000 to 300,000 steps.

Capturing a whole room from inside

A room works, and needs no mask, because everything in it belongs to the scene. The original NeRF assumes the scene lies between a near and a far distance; Mip-NeRF 360 and Gaussian splatting handle scenes that surround the camera, and their test scenes include indoor rooms.

The camera has to walk, here a slow loop at two heights: turning on one spot gives no depth, because nothing shifts against the background. Mirrors, windows and plain white walls stay hard for COLMAP and for both methods.

Barron et al. 2022 (Mip-NeRF 360: unbounded scenes; its dataset includes the indoor scenes room, kitchen, counter and bonsai); Kerbl et al. 2023, Table 1 and Fig. 5 (Dr Johnson, an indoor scene from Hedman et al. 2018).

A question I asked myself after the globe: could I capture my whole room the same way? Yes, it is a standard case. Left, what I did: an object in the middle, cameras on rings around it, all looking in. Right, a room: the cameras are inside and look outward, so every direction has a wall at a different distance. The original NeRF struggles here, because it samples rays between a fixed near and far distance and spends its capacity evenly; Mip-NeRF 360 contracts far space so it fits, and Gaussian splatting simply places Gaussians wherever COLMAP found points. The Mip-NeRF 360 dataset that the Gaussian splatting results used has four indoor scenes. No mask is needed, the room is the scene. How to film it: walk a slow loop through the room, filming the walls and furniture, at two heights, with the same fixed exposure and fast shutter as the globe. Do not stand in the middle and turn: from one spot, near and far things do not move against each other, so neither COLMAP nor NeRF can recover depth. Mirrors and windows change with the viewpoint, and blank walls give COLMAP nothing to match.

Summary

5MB

per scene: the scene is stored as two small networks that give density and colour for any point and direction.

+5.0dB

over the best earlier method on the realistic synthetic scenes, 31.0 against 26.1 dB, and the highest PSNR on all three test sets.

15s

of Instant-NGP training pass NeRF's quality on its own synthetic scenes (31.4 against 31.0 dB), with a learned hash grid and a tiny network.

134fps

for 3D Gaussian splatting on large real scenes, close to Mip-NeRF 360's quality (27.2 against 27.7 dB), at 734 MB per scene.

Mildenhall et al. 2020, Table 1 (Realistic Synthetic 360°: NeRF 31.01 dB, Neural Volumes 26.05 dB) and Sec. 6.3; Müller et al. 2022, Table 2; Kerbl et al. 2023, Table 1 (Mip-NeRF 360 dataset). Last row: my capture, 32 held-out photos, both methods on the same laptop GPU.

Four numbers to remember. First, what NeRF is: a scene stored as a function, two networks, 5 MB, with density from position and colour from position and direction, rendered by the volume rendering sum. Second, how well it works: about 5 dB above the previous best on the hard synthetic scenes, roughly a third of the squared error, and best PSNR everywhere. Third, Instant-NGP: 15 seconds of training reach NeRF's quality, because most of the work moves from a big network into learned hash grids. Fourth, Gaussian splatting: no network, real-time rendering at near the best quality, paid for in memory. Fifth, my own capture confirms it on a small scale: on the same photos, Gaussian splatting trained in half an hour instead of nine hours, renders thousands of times faster, and scores slightly better, while NeRF holds up better where few photos look.
