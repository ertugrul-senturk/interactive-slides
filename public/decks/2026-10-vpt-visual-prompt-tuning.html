Full fine-tuning stores a new copy of the model per task

Full fine-tuning retrains every weight of the pretrained model on the new task, and it is usually the most accurate option. The cost is that each task keeps its own retrained copy, so storage grows with every task and with the size of the model.

In 2020, a prompt was text. GPT-3, a frozen model with 175 billion weights, was only trained to continue text, yet it does a new task when you type a description of the task in front of the input. Not a single weight changes. But words are discrete, so the only way to improve a prompt is trial and error.

In 2021, prompt tuning (Lester et al.) replaced the words with learned vectors that correspond to no word. They are trained by gradient descent on the task's examples while the model stays frozen, and they are all you store per task. Prefix-tuning (Li & Liang) does the same at every layer.

The sentiment example is illustrative. Brown et al. 2020 (GPT-3); Lester, Al-Rfou, Constant 2021 (prompt tuning, T5); Li & Liang 2021 (prefix-tuning).

Two steps, one idea. In 2020, GPT-3 showed that a frozen language model does new tasks if you type a description of the task in front of the input; that text is the prompt, and no weight changes. Its weakness: words are discrete, so you can only improve a prompt by trial and error. In 2021, prompt tuning kept the frozen model and replaced the words with vectors trained by gradient descent: the red squares correspond to no word at all. Per task you store k vectors of 1,024 numbers, roughly 20,000 numbers instead of billions. Prefix-tuning does the same at every layer. LoRA, the next talk, also dates from 2021 but takes a different route: it changes the weights, not the input.

How a Vision Transformer reads an image

A fixed grid cuts the image into 196 patches of 16 × 16 pixels, and each patch, read row by row, becomes a vector of 768 numbers. One extra vector, [CLS], goes in front to collect a summary of the whole image: the 12 layers mix all 197 vectors, and the head reads only [CLS] to name the class.

The layers only see a sequence of vectors, so we can add a few more.

ViT-B/16: 12 layers, vectors of 768 numbers, 85.8 million weights. Dosovitskiy et al., ICLR 2021. Image: a melanoma from DermaMNIST, 224 × 224 pixels.

Spend a minute here, it pays off. Walk the picture from left to right and up: a fixed grid cuts the image into 14 by 14 patches, whatever the image shows; nothing is selected, all 196 go in, read row by row from patch 1 at the top left to 196 at the bottom right. Each patch, 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 pixel values, goes through one linear layer to become a grey vector of 768 numbers (the two 768s are a coincidence of ViT-B), with a position vector added so the model knows where it came from. In front sits one extra learned vector, [CLS], which carries no image content and collects a summary as it goes up. The 12 Transformer layers, blue because they will stay frozen in VPT, let every vector look at every other one. At the top, only the [CLS] vector goes to the head, the one task-specific part, which outputs melanoma. The point for VPT: the Transformer does not care how long the sequence is or where a vector came from, so we can add our own vectors.

The grey vectors come from the input, as before. The red ones do not: they start as random numbers, are trained on the task's examples while the model stays frozen, and are then put in front of every input of that task. A visual prompt is therefore not text and not a pattern painted on the pixels, only learned numbers in the model's input space.

In language, prompt tuning matched full fine-tuning only once the model had about 10 billion weights. VPT beats full fine-tuning on 20 of 24 tasks with an 86-million-weight ViT-B.

Lester et al. 2021 (T5); Jia et al. 2022, Table 1. Not to be confused with CoOp (Zhou et al. 2021, text prompts for CLIP) or Bahng et al. 2022 (a pixel pattern).

The one-sentence version: VPT is prompt tuning for a Vision Transformer. Both rows learn vectors in the model's input space and leave the model alone; the only difference is where the other vectors come from, words or image patches. Say plainly what it is not: not the text prompts for CLIP that CoOp learns, and not a learned pattern painted on the pixels as in Bahng et al.; the paper tests the pixel version too and it is clearly weaker. Then the surprise: in language, learned prompts needed about 10 billion weights to match full fine-tuning; VPT beats it on 20 of 24 tasks with 86 million. The authors themselves suggest vision prompts may behave differently from language prompts. Point at the labels under each row: the grey vectors come from the input, the red ones never do; they start random, are trained on the task, and the same set goes in front of every image of that task.

VPT trains a few extra input vectors and the head

Figures: Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 2. Equations (6) and (8) of the paper: x class token, E patch vectors, P prompts, Z the prompt positions after a layer, Li layer i.

Shallow first: p red vectors are put in front of the patch vectors once, before layer 1. They then travel through all 12 frozen layers like any other token; the paper calls their outputs Z. Only P and the head are trained, about 0.14% of the model for 50 prompts and a 102-class head. Press the arrow key for deep: every layer gets its own fresh set of p vectors, and whatever came out of the previous layer at those positions is thrown away, the blank in the equation. Twelve times the parameters, still only half a percent of the model, and deep is the variant that beats full fine-tuning. Position does not matter because prompts are added after the position embedding.

Why a few extra tokens can steer a frozen network

In every layer, each token takes in information from all the other tokens; this is attention. With prompts in the sequence, [CLS] and every patch also take in information from the prompts.

So training the prompts changes what the frozen network computes, without changing a single weight.

Mechanism: Sec. 3 of Jia et al. 2022; Appendix B relates it to prefix-tuning ("VPT-prefix"). Line widths and the red share of [CLS] are illustrative, not measured.

The intuition people need: a frozen function can still be steered if you control part of its input. Start without prompts: inside a frozen layer, the [CLS] token collects a mix of the patch tokens. Press the arrow key: the red prompts join the sequence, and now part of what [CLS] collects comes from them. Every other token sees the prompts too, at every layer. No weight changes, and nothing is read from the prompt outputs themselves; in VPT-deep they are discarded after each layer. If asked: the paper points out this is equivalent to prepending learned keys and values inside attention, which is what prefix-tuning does in NLP.

VPT in code: a few lines and one new hyperparameter

# VPT-deep on a frozen ViT-B/16, simplified from demo/vpt/vpt_model.pyvit = timm.create_model(BACKBONE, pretrained=True)                # ImageNet-21k ViT-B/16vit.requires_grad_(False)                                         # backbone frozenprompts = nn.Parameter(torch.empty(12, p, 768).uniform_(-b, b))   # trained: 12 layers × p × 768head = nn.Linear(768, num_classes)                                # trained: the classifier def forward(img):    x = vit._pos_embed(vit.patch_embed(img))                      # [CLS] + 196 patch vectors    for i, layer in enumerate(vit.blocks):        rest = x[:, 1:] if i == 0 else x[:, 1 + p:]               # drop last layer's prompts        P = prompts[i].expand(len(x), -1, -1)                     # this layer's prompts        x = layer(torch.cat([x[:, :1], P, rest], dim=1))          # frozen layer on [CLS, P, rest]    return head(vit.norm(x)[:, 0])                                # classify from [CLS]

The prompts start random, with Xavier-uniform values as in the official code. The paper tried smarter starts, such as class prototypes, and random did as well or better.

Training is plain SGD with momentum for 100 epochs on a cosine schedule. Learning rate and weight decay are picked on a validation split, for every method alike.

The one new hyperparameter is the prompt length p, chosen per task from {1, 5, 10, 50, 100, 200} on the validation split.

Recipe: Jia et al. 2022, Sec. 4.1 and Appendix A. Code is my re-implementation, which reproduces the paper's Oxford Flowers result (99.1% vs the paper's 99.0%).

The code is there to make it concrete, not to read line by line. The red lines are the whole method: a prompt tensor of 12 × p × 768 numbers, a head, and three lines inside the layer loop. The first of those slices away what the previous layer produced at the prompt positions, then this layer's fresh prompts go in next to [CLS] and the patches, and the frozen layer runs as usual. Underneath: random initialisation is fine, the optimiser is standard SGD, and the only new knob is p, picked on validation.

Four places to put the prompt

Numbers from Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 5 (ViT-B/16, prompts in the first layer only); "All four" is the mean of the three groups. Diagrams redrawn.

The question: where should the learned numbers enter the network? Two places in principle: in the embedding space, as vectors next to the patch vectors, or in pixel space, as part of the image before the embedding layer. Prepend is the default we have seen: extra tokens after the embedding. Add: the prompts are added onto patch vectors, so the sequence is not longer; it loses in all three groups, 8 points on Structured, and the deep version loses too (43.2 against 55.0 on Structured). Prepend-pixel: learned pixel patches next to the image patches, then the frozen embedding layer; loses everywhere, 5 points on Natural. Concat-channel: a learned fourth channel on the image, the embedding layer trained to read it; it collapses, 30 points lower on Natural and below even a linear probe there (68.9). Summary: separate tokens in the embedding space are the right place. All numbers are VPT-shallow because that is the only setting where the paper tests all four options.

Where VPT falls short

Be honest here; it is what a reading group is for. Three settings where the main result does not carry over. First, the pretraining. Everything so far used a ViT-B pretrained with labels on ImageNet-21k. The paper repeats VTAB-1k with the same architecture pretrained without labels on ImageNet-1k, by MoCo v3 (contrastive) and by MAE (masked autoencoder). The chart shows the mean of the three VTAB group averages. With labels, VPT-deep leads full fine-tuning, 72.0 against 69.0. With MoCo v3 it is 65.2 against 69.6, and Bias (69.2), Partial-1 (68.3) and the adapter (68.2) are all ahead of it. With MAE it collapses to 41.1, closer to the linear probe at 32.1 than to full fine-tuning at 64.3, while Partial-1, which retrains only the last block, reaches 61.5. The authors only say these self-supervised ViTs are fundamentally different and leave the reason open. It connects to the MAE paper from last time: MAE features are known to probe badly, and a few input tokens apparently cannot reorganise them the way retraining the last block can. Press the arrow key for Swin, a hierarchical Transformer that attends within local windows and merges patches in deeper layers; the prompts sit in the windows and are dropped at patch merging. VPT still beats the other cheap methods (Bias, linear probe, Partial, MLP-3) in every group, but full fine-tuning is ahead of VPT-deep in all three, by 6.3 points on Structured. Oddly, VPT-shallow does slightly better than full fine-tuning on Natural, 79.9 against 79.1; it is left out of the chart to keep it simple. Press again for segmentation: ADE20K, 150 classes per pixel, SETR on a ViT-L pretrained on ImageNet-21k; mIoU is the mean overlap between predicted and true regions. The decoder head is trained in every method, which is why even "head only" trains 13 million weights. VPT-deep gets 42.1 against 48.3, a little below Bias at 43.4; prompts plus biases give 44.0, within 1.5 of a fully fine-tuned ResNet-101 with four times the trained weights. This is also where LoRA will come in: it edits weights, so it does not depend on the input space being "steerable".

VPT saves storage, not training memory or inference time

Easy to miss in the main paper: the saving is disk, not GPU. Three arrow-key steps, same rows each time: full fine-tuning in dark with the dashed line at its value, the cheap baselines in grey, VPT-deep in red with 1, 50, 100 and 200 prompts per layer. Storage: a task keeps only what it trained, so VPT-deep is 0.35 MB with one prompt per layer and 7.7 MB with 200, against 343 MB for a full copy; bias and adapter are as small, so storage is what all these methods buy, and it is VPT's real win. Training memory: no saving. The prompts in the first layer only get a gradient if it travels back through all twelve frozen layers, so their activations are kept, as in full fine-tuning; with 200 prompts the longer sequence makes it 25.8 GB against 11.7. Only the linear probe, 0.9 GB, avoids this. Training time per image is not shown: one prompt trains faster than full fine-tuning, 214 against 359 ms, but 200 prompts are as slow, 360 ms. Inference time: every test image carries the prompts through all twelve layers. Fifty prompts take the sequence from 197 to 247 tokens and the time from 70 to 82 ms; 200 prompts double it, 141 ms. The paper notes this is far from the 4x one would expect from quadratic attention. The prompts cannot be folded into the weights, so the cost stays; the paper's prefix variant, which puts the prompts only into the keys and values, recovers some of it (Fig. 19). LoRA, next time, merges its update into the weights and costs nothing at test time. If asked: the table has only 1 and 200 prompts; the 50 and 100 values are read off Fig. 18's plotted points, and the 1 and 200 points of that figure match the table. If someone asks "why not just use this for everything", this is the answer.

Each method was trained once with 1,000 images and once with the full training set; there are no points in between.

Step through the three datasets with the arrow keys. Each tab has only two measured settings, 1,000 training images and the full training set, so they are drawn as two separate groups of bars, not as a line. The score is balanced accuracy on the test set, the mean recall over classes, because skin lesions are so imbalanced. Skin lesions: VPT is 5 points above the linear probe with 1,000 images and 13 points above with all 7,007, because the prompts can reshape the features and a linear head cannot; full fine-tuning stays 2 to 3 points ahead. Blood cells: everything is above 96, and VPT and full fine-tuning are within a few tenths of a point of each other. Chest X-rays: with 1,000 images VPT barely beats the linear probe and trails full fine-tuning by almost 4 points; with all 4,708 it comes out on top. So across all six settings VPT never loses to the linear probe and is never more than 4 points behind full fine-tuning. Unlike the paper, the low-data setting did not favour VPT here; medical images behave like the paper's Specialized group.

What each method costs on skin lesions

Three steps, one cost each, all measured on my laptop for skin lesions trained on the full training split; every number on the slide is read from the training logs and the benchmark file. Storage first: this is the paper's argument, and it holds. Linear probe and VPT keep one copy of the base model and add a small file per task, kilobytes or about 2 MB, while full fine-tuning needs a whole new 343 MB model per task; the accuracy panel is the price, a few points below full fine-tuning but far above the linear probe. Training: VPT is cheaper than full fine-tuning but much more expensive than the linear probe, because the error signal has to travel back through all twelve frozen layers to reach the prompts, so the activations must be kept. That is the same pattern as the paper's Table 12 on an A100. Inference: VPT is the only method that is slower than the original model, because every layer processes 50 extra tokens, and unlike a weight update the prompts cannot be merged into the model after training. Linear probe and full fine-tuning run the same network, so their small difference is noise. Accuracy for all three tasks and both data sizes is on the previous slide.

Jia et al. 2022, Table 1 (24 tasks, ViT-B/16) and Table 12 (memory, test time); 0.6% is VPT-deep with 50 prompts and a 102-class head. Medical datasets: my runs, balanced accuracy on the test set.

This is the slide to leave up while summing up and taking questions: four points, each with the one number to remember. First, what a visual prompt is. Not text and not pixels, but learned vectors among the patch tokens of a frozen ViT; with 50 prompts at every layer and the head that is about 0.6% of the weights. Second, the headline: on a ViT-B pretrained with labels, VPT-deep beat full fine-tuning on 20 of the 24 tasks, and all 24 tasks together cost 1.18 models of storage instead of 24. Third, the limits: it saves storage only, 99.4% less per task than a fully fine-tuned copy. Training memory is about the same as full fine-tuning, inference is slower because the sequence is longer, and on self-supervised backbones it loses, 23 points behind on MAE. That is the opening for LoRA next time. Fourth, my own runs: averaged over the three medical datasets and both training-set sizes, VPT-deep is about a point behind full fine-tuning and clearly ahead of the linear probe, as the demo showed.
