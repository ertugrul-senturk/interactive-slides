Adapting a frozen vision Transformer by learning a handful of extra input tokens

Menglin Jia, Luming Tang, Bor-Chun Chen, Claire Cardie, Serge Belongie, Bharath Hariharan, Ser-Nam Lim
Cornell University · Meta AI · University of Copenhagen · ECCV 2022

This is the first paper in a short series on parameter-efficient fine-tuning. VPT is the vision paper that took prompt tuning from NLP and asked whether it works for image encoders. Spoiler: for supervised ViTs it works surprisingly well, and at the end I will run a model I trained with it.

This is the practical motivation. Each square on the left is a full ViT-Base. Fine-tune it on 24 tasks and you ship 24 of them. VPT keeps one frozen copy and stores only the task-specific bits, which for all 24 tasks together are about a fifth of one backbone. The question the paper asks is whether we can get this saving without losing accuracy.

The cheaper options tune only part of the model, and they give up accuracy

tunedfrozen Number: mean accuracy over the four task groups (FGVC, VTAB Natural, Specialized, Structured)

ViT-B/16 pre-trained on ImageNet-21k, from Jia et al. 2022, Table 1. Bias and Adapter were popularized in NLP (BitFit; Houlsby et al.).

Head-oriented methods treat the backbone as a feature extractor. Linear probing is the cheapest and the weakest. Backbone-oriented methods touch the inside: Bias tunes only the bias terms, Adapter inserts small bottleneck MLPs. Bias gets close to Full, but nothing here beats it. Keep 73.9 for Full in mind.

The prompt becomes continuous vectors trained by gradient descent. Per task you store a few thousand numbers instead of the model, about 1000× less. In NLP it matches full fine-tuning only for very large models.

Does the same idea work for an image encoder?

Hand-written prompts are discrete and brittle. Prompt tuning replaces them with vectors in the embedding space and learns them. The open question in 2022 was whether this transfers to vision, where the inputs are patches, not words. Click to reveal the question.

VPT prepends p learnable tokens and freezes everything else

learned promptcomputed, not storedfrozen
Shallow: one set of p prompts goes in before layer 1. After that, the outputs at those positions, Z, are just activations. Deep: every layer gets its own fresh prompts, and the outputs at prompt positions are thrown away, which is the underscore in the equation. Prompts enter after the position embedding, so where they sit in the sequence does not matter. Only the red parts and the head are trained.

VTAB-1k is the important part: only 1,000 training images per task, which is the low-data regime where efficient methods tend to shine. Note that p is tuned per task. We come back to that.

Prompts work best as extra tokens in embedding space

Jia et al. 2022, Fig. 5. Add: summed onto the patch embeddings instead of prepended. Prepend-pixel / Concat-channel: learned pixels or an extra input channel before the patch embedding.

Two lessons. First, prepending beats adding: keeping the patch tokens untouched and giving attention extra tokens to look at is what matters. Second, prompts in pixel space are much worse. Concatenating a channel loses about 30 points on Natural. The latent space after the embedding is where task information is easiest to inject.

On self-supervised backbones the advantage disappears

Mean over VTAB Natural, Specialized and Structured, ViT-B/16. Supervised: Jia et al. 2022, Table 1. MoCo v3 and MAE: Table 4.

This is the slide I find most interesting, and it connects to the MAE paper from last time. On the supervised backbone VPT-deep is to the right of Full. On MoCo v3 it falls behind, and on MAE it collapses, close to linear probing, while Partial-1 almost matches Full. The authors leave the reason open. One hypothesis: MAE features are not linearly separable, as MAE's own linear-probing numbers show, and a few input tokens cannot reorganize them the way tuning the last block can.

This is easy to miss in the main paper. The saving is in storage, not in training cost. At p = 200 training memory is more than double Full's, and inference is twice as slow. LoRA, which comes next, keeps the storage benefit but can be merged into the weights, so it adds nothing at inference.

Storage per task is the paper's argument: kilobytes, megabytes, or a whole new 343 MB model. Training memory is the paper's Table 12 point: VPT still backpropagates through every frozen layer, so it costs much more than a linear probe. Four things I would ask the authors

How much is the tuning budget?

Per task, VPT searches 6 prompt lengths × 10 learning rates × 4 weight decays on val, 240 runs. Full gets 4 × 4 = 16.

Why does it fail on MAE?

The gap to Full is −23 points on MAE Natural. The paper says it is an open question. Is it the features, or the optimization?

Is storage the right currency?

Training memory and compute stay at Full's level, and inference gets slower. The win is per-task disk space and multi-task serving.

What do the prompts encode?

The prompts are never interpreted. Deep prompts act like extra keys and values at every layer (the paper's VPT-prefix), close to prefix-tuning in NLP.

Good questions for discussion. The tuning budget point is fair but not fatal, because baselines were also grid-searched. The MAE result is the real open problem. And the prefix view is the bridge to LoRA: both change attention, one through extra tokens, the other through low-rank weight updates.

What to remember

Learn a few tokens at the input of every layer and freeze the rest.0.5–2% extra parameters per task, one shared backbone.

On supervised ViTs it beats full fine-tuning on 20 of 24 tasks.Largest gains on low-data tasks that are far from ImageNet.

It saves storage, not compute, and it does not carry over to MAE.This is the opening for methods that merge into the weights, such as LoRA.

On three medical tasks it always beat the linear probe and stayed within 4 points of full fine-tuning.At 1.9 MB per task instead of 343 MB, on one shared base model.

Three sentences. The third one sets up the rest of the session.
